### The Ol Chiki Script
Unlike many indigenous languages that use modified versions of Latin, Devanagari, or Bengali scripts, Santali has its own unique script called Ol Chiki.

* Creation: It was invented in 1925 by Pandit Raghunath Murmu to accurately represent Santali phonemes that could not be easily written in Indo-Aryan or European alphabets.
* Graphic Conception: Every letter is shaped after a real-world object or action from daily life, matching the first sound of that object's name:
* Ol (the letter 'O') is shaped like a hand writing.
   * At (the letter 'T') is shaped like the earth.
   * Ak (the letter 'K') is shaped like a flying bird's wing.
* Symmetry: Unlike regional Indian scripts, it is not an abugida (where vowels are marked as accents around consonants). It is a true alphabet written from left to right, where vowels and consonants carry equal structural weight.

### Grammatical Structure of Santali
Santali is highly complex, featuring grammatical elements entirely alien to Indo-Aryan languages like Hindi or Bengali.

### 1. Pronoun and Number System (Three-Way Split)
While Indo-Aryan languages only use singular and plural, Santali uses a strict three-way grammatical number system: Singular, Dual (exactly two), and Plural (three or more).
Furthermore, the first-person plural pronouns split based on Inclusion vs. Exclusion:

* Ālaṅ: "We" (You and I—inclusive).
* Āliñ: "We" (He/She and I, but not you—exclusive).
* Ābo: "We" (All of us—inclusive).
* Āle: "We" (They and I, but not you—exclusive).

### 2. Noun Classification (Animate vs. Inanimate)
Instead of masculine and feminine genders, Santali groups the universe into living and non-living entities. Verbs dynamically adjust their suffixes depending on whether the subject or object is animate (humans, animals, spirits) or inanimate (rocks, plants, tools).

### 3. Extensive Agglutination and Infixes
Words are constructed by pasting long chains of prefixes, suffixes, and infixes (inserting sounds right into the middle of a root word) to change meaning.

* Example of an Infix: Take the root word dal (to strike/hit).
* If you insert the reciprocal infix -p-, it shifts the root into a mutual action: da-p-al (to fight each other).
   * If you add a noun-forming infix -n-, it becomes da-n-al (the act of hitting, or a weapon used for hitting).

### Step-by-Step Sentence Construction
To understand how Santali builds a sentence compared to Indo-Aryan languages like Hindi or English, let's break down a simple thought: "We (two) are hitting them (three or more)."
In English or Hindi, this requires separate words for pronouns, tense, and plurality. In Santali, almost the entire grammatical context is glued directly onto the verb root using agglutination.

### The Core Components

   1. Verb Root: dal (to strike/hit)
   2. Subject Pronoun: ālañ (We two, inclusive)
   3. Present Continuous Tense Marker: -et'
   4. Animate Object Suffix (Plural): -ko (them)
   5. Finite Sentence Marker: -a

### Putting It Together
The verb, tense, object, and sentence markers fuse into a single complex block:
$$\text{dal (hit)} + \text{et' (doing)} + \text{ko (them)} + \text{a (marker)} = \textbf{daletkoa}$$ 
When combined with the subject pronoun, the complete sentence is:

Ālañ daletkoa.
(Literal translation: You-and-I hitting-them-are.)

### What Happens if the Context Changes?
If the sentence structure alters even slightly, the fused verb block dynamically morphs:

- Change the object from "them" (plural) to "him/her" (singular -e):

$$\text{Ālañ } \textbf{daledea} \rightarrow \text{"We two are hitting him."}$$

- Change the tense from present to past (-ked):

$$\text{Ālañ } \textbf{dalketkoa} \rightarrow \text{"We two hit them."}$$

This radical morphing of a single verb root highlights just how structurally distinct Austroasiatic languages are from the Indo-Aryan family tree.
